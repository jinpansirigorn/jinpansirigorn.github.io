SQL + BUSINESS ANALYTICS

TuneWorks Entertainment Analytics

Using a relational database to move from raw bookings to customer, agent, and revenue decisions.

THE QUESTION

What can transactional data reveal about customers, agents, and revenue?

TuneWorks is a fictional entertainment agency with booking, customer, agent, entertainer, and preference data but no in-house analytics function. The project treated the database like a consulting engagement: understand the schema, assess data quality, generate SQL-based insights, and recommend actions for leadership.

ANSWER IN ONE MINUTE

The most useful insight came from a mismatch between what customers said they liked and what they actually booked.

That mismatch changed the business question from “Which genre should we market?” to “What actually drives a booking: availability, price, popularity, or stale preference data?”

DATABASE THINKING

The analysis depended on relationships, not one flat table.

Engagements acted as the core transaction table connecting customers, agents, and entertainers. Preference and style tables added many-to-many relationships needed to compare stated preferences with observed bookings.

CUSTOMER INSIGHT

The highest-value customer behaved very differently from the recorded profile.

$25,585highest customer spend
87.5%of Elizabeth's bookings outside recorded preferences
>50%non-preferred bookings for every customer in the analysis

This does not prove that preference is irrelevant. It suggests the stored preference field is a weak standalone marketing signal and creates a better follow-up question: is the mismatch caused by stale preferences, limited entertainer supply, price, availability, or popularity?

SQL DEPTH

The queries moved from description to behavioral metrics.

Window functions

LAG() calculated time between bookings by agent and by customer.

CTEs

Combined customer spending, recency, and booking intervals into reusable analysis steps.

Multi-table joins

Connected engagements, entertainer styles, and customer preferences to test behavioral fit.

Time analysis

Used calendar tables to summarize monthly bookings, revenue, and average contract price.

OPERATING INSIGHT

Agent totals hid different productivity and concentration profiles.

Combining commission with average time between bookings helped distinguish high-performing agents from agents with longer booking intervals and weaker commission. Looking at commission by customer also exposed concentration risk: similar total performance can depend on very different customer portfolios.

Carol · strong performance

Second-highest commission with the shortest average booking interval in the analysis.

Scott / Caleb · investigate

Longer average intervals and comparatively low commission suggested a need for support or performance review.

John / Scott · concentration risk

Greater dependence on large customers made retention especially important.

Karen / Carol / Marianne · diversified

Commission was spread across more customers, creating a more resilient portfolio.

BUSINESS ACTION

Turn the analysis into different actions for different problems.

Customer retention

Use value and booking frequency to tailor retention offers rather than treating every customer the same.

Preference data

Do not rely on stated genre alone; collect or test behavioral drivers such as availability, price, and browsing behavior.

Agent management

Review compensation and support using both productivity and client-concentration measures.

Data collection

Add behavioral and booking-context fields so future analysis can explain why customers choose substitutes.
